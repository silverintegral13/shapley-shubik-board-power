1. The university council (assignment)

Eleven seats: Students 4, Staff 3, Faculty 2, Alumni 1, Corporate 1. Routine matters need a simple majority (6 of 11). Operational matters also need a majority of Faculty, which with two faculty members means both. Strategic matters also need a majority of Students (3 of 4).

Q3 · What the numbers say

  • Seats are not power. On routine matters Students hold 36% of seats but 40% of the power as a bloc. Alumni and Corporate hold 9% each but only 6.7%.
  • Concurrent-majority clauses create vetoes. Requiring a Faculty majority turns 2 seats (18%) into 61.7% of operational power. Adding a Student majority for strategic matters leaves Students and Faculty with 50% each. Staff, Alumni and Corporate become dummies: they have 45% of the seats and zero power, because their votes can never change an outcome.
  • Cohesion helps big factions and can hurt veto factions. Bloc voting raises Students' routine power from 36.4% to 40%. Under the operational rule, though, Faculty are stronger split (81.8%) than united (61.7%), because each faculty member becomes a separate veto player. A "majority of two" is really a unanimity requirement, and it hands every member a personal veto. The same arithmetic is at the heart of the Tata dispute below.
  • Design implication. Use faction-majority clauses only where a group's consent is really needed. Avoid even-sized veto groups of two. Make sure no stakeholder becomes a permanent dummy, since a member whose vote never matters has little reason to engage. A casting vote or odd-sized sub-groups can soften these effects.

2. Tata Sons: how Tata Trusts controls the board, and why it is now contested

The structure

The Tata Trusts own about 66% of Tata Sons, the group's unlisted holding company. The Shapoorji Pallonji (SP) group owns about 18.4%, group companies about 12.9%, and individuals about 2.9%. The Trusts' 66% is not held by one entity: Sir Dorabji Tata Trust holds about 28% and Sir Ratan Tata Trust about 23.6%.

The Trusts' control over the board comes from two clauses in Tata Sons' Articles of Association, added between 2012 and 2014 and upheld by the Supreme Court in the 2021 Cyrus Mistry judgment:

  • Article 104B lets the Trusts nominate up to one-third of the directors.
  • Article 121 says that any board matter decided by a majority also needs the affirmative vote of a majority of the 104B (Trust-nominated) directors present. Commentators call this a "double-barrelled" majority.

In Shapley-Shubik terms, Article 121 is a second winning condition: board majority AND majority of Trust nominees. That is exactly the Operational/Strategic rule from the assignment.

The 2026 dispute in brief

On 17 September 2026 the six-member board (Chairman N. Chandrasekaran, CFO Saurabh Agrawal, independents Harish Manwani and Anita Marangoly George, and Trust nominees Noel Tata and Venu Srinivasan) voted 4-1 to give Chandrasekaran another five-year term. Chandrasekaran recused himself. Noel Tata voted against. Venu Srinivasan voted for, even though one of the Trusts had tried to stop him attending and voting. The Trusts call the resolution a legal nullity: with two nominees, a "majority of nominees" needs both. Tata Sons relied on a casting vote to resolve the 1-1 split between the nominees. An earlier opinion by Justice R.F. Nariman had already held that nominee directors must use their own judgment and cannot be bound to vote as the Trusts instruct. Both sides held talks in late October, and the matter returns to the board on 26 November 2026.

What the Trusts' seats are worth

How the Trusts exert control. Without Article 121, two seats out of six give the Trusts' nominees one-third of the power, no more than any other pair of directors. Article 121 makes the nominee bloc a veto player: nothing passes without it. That lifts the Trusts to 58.3% of board power from 33% of the seats. On a three-nominee board it was 75% from 43% of the seats. On top of that, the Trusts' 66% shareholding makes them a dictator at the shareholder level (power 1.0 on ordinary resolutions; see below). They pick who sits on the board and hold a veto over what it decides.

Why a 1-1 split matters so much: power vs nominee loyalty

The Trusts can nominate directors, but they cannot instruct them. So the useful measure of "internal strength" here is p, the probability that each nominee follows the Trusts' line. The solid lines show the expected power exercised on the Trusts' behalf: the bloc of nominees who follow them. The dashed line shows the two nominees' combined personal power.

  • With full loyalty (p = 1) both readings give the Trusts 58.3%. The dispute only bites when the nominees split.
  • At p = 0.5, about where the Trusts stand when one nominee sides with management, the Trusts' line is worth under their own reading of Article 121 but only under Tata Sons' casting-vote reading. Under the Trusts' reading each nominee individually holds a veto (38.3% each, see table below), so Noel Tata's single "no" blocks the reappointment. Under Tata Sons' reading that veto is gone.
  • The dashed line falls as p rises: two independent veto-holders have more combined power (76.7%) than one united bloc (58.3%). That power belongs to the individuals, though, not to the Trusts. This is why the Trusts have spent 2025-26 trying to raise p: annual reappointment of nominees over 75, a proposed protocol requiring nominees to consult the trustees, and an attempt to withdraw Srinivasan's authority to vote.

3. Anthropic: the S-1 two-key structure

The structure (Form S-1, filed 26 October 2026)

  • Board: seven authorised directors. Four are Class T directors, elected and removed by the Long-Term Benefit Trust (LTBT) as sole holder of Class T stock: Reed Hastings, Vas Narasimhan, Chris Liddell and one vacancy. The other three (Dario Amodei, Daniela Amodei as Chair, and a third director) are elected by Class A and Class F holders voting together.
  • Stockholders: a single Class F share held by the Founder LLC carries 50.1% of total voting power on general matters. The LLC votes it as a majority of the seven co-founders directs. If they can't agree, the share votes proportionally with Class A. Public Class A stock has one vote per share. Class N (mainly strategic partners such as Amazon and Google) has no general vote. Class T has no economic rights and votes only on its own directors and a few protective matters.
  • Protected matters: some bylaw provisions need 80% of the Whole Board (6 of 7). Changing the LTBT or the Class T rights needs the Trust Amendment Threshold. With the Trustees in favour, that means 80% of authorised directors and 80% of the Trustees in office. There is also a stockholder route without the Trustees, which needs the Class F share (co-founder unanimity in the first three years), employee Class E and Class A approval. After the Class F sunset it needs 85% of Class A.

Board power

How power works. With all four Class T seats filled and those directors acting together, the LTBT-elected bloc is a dictator on ordinary board resolutions (power 1.0 from 57% of seats). The founder-side directors' votes never change an outcome. Today, with one Class T seat vacant, each side has three directors and a majority of the Whole Board still needs four, so each side holds a veto and power is 50/50. Filling the fourth seat is worth half the board's power to the LTBT. On 80%-of-board matters it is 50/50 whatever happens.

As with Tata, the guardian's power runs through directors who owe their own fiduciary duties. The S-1 says the Trust and its Trustees take no part in day-to-day management or strategy. So the relevant "internal strength" is how reliably the Class T directors vote together:

  • If the Class T directors act independently while the founder-side directors stay united, the founder-side bloc gets 60%, more than its seat share. A cohesive minority beats a fragmented majority.
  • With the vacancy (dashed line), the Class T directors cannot get above 50% however united they are.

Amending the Trust itself

Under the Trust Amendment Threshold (route i), the Class T directors, the founder-side directors and the Trustees are each veto players: one-third each as blocs. The LTBT's protection does not rest on its board majority alone. Changing its rights needs the founder-side directors and the Trustees as well. The alternative stockholder route requires Founder LLC unanimity for the first three years, a cohesion requirement of p = 1 across all seven co-founders.

Stockholder level: the Founder LLC as a cohesion device

Because a single share carries 50.1% of the vote, the Founder LLC is a dictator on general stockholder matters, including electing the three non-Class T directors. Public Class A, with 49.9% of the votes, has zero Shapley-Shubik power. The LLC wrapper matters: if the seven co-founders voted their slices separately, Class A would hold 75% of the power. The LLC turns seven people into one guaranteed bloc, and inside it each co-founder has a one-seventh say.

Summary. Anthropic runs a two-key system. The LTBT holds the key to ordinary board decisions once its four seats are filled. The Founder LLC holds the key to stockholder votes and the other three seats. The most important changes, to protected bylaws and to the Trust itself, need both keys.

4. Lessons for board design

  • Control comes from rules, not shares. The Tata Trusts own 66% of Tata Sons, but their board control comes from Articles 104B and 121. Anthropic's LTBT owns no economic interest at all and still elects the board majority.
  • A veto from a group of two is a personal veto. The assignment's faculty, Tata's two nominees and Anthropic's 3-3 board with a vacancy all show it: even-sized blocs and "majority of two" clauses split power in ways the drafters may not have meant.
  • Guardians depend on loyalty they cannot command. Both structures give power to directors who must exercise their own judgment. Power vs p is the right way to judge such a design, not the seat count.
  • Cohesion devices are power devices. The Founder LLC's single share, Tata's nominee protocols and bloc-voting agreements all raise p. A small cohesive bloc can outweigh a larger fragmented one.
  • Spell out tie-breaks. The Tata dispute is about whether a casting vote can cure a split inside the protected class. Writing that down would have settled what the dispute is about: whether the Trusts' power is 58% or 40%.

Method

Shapley-Shubik index

Line the players up in every possible order. Each one says "yes" in turn, and the pivotal player is the one whose vote first makes the motion pass. A player's index is the share of the n! orders in which they are pivotal. The equivalent subset form, used by the engine:

φi = ΣS ⊆ N∖{i} |S|! (n − |S| − 1)! / n! · [ v(S ∪ {i}) − v(S) ]

Here v(S) = 1 if the yes-voters in S satisfy every condition of the rule, and 0 otherwise. The indices add up to 1. This is the same calculation as the class spreadsheet, which lists the orderings and counts pivots (the Excel download includes that view for up to 8 players).

Players, members and coalitions

  • A player is a group of members who vote together. A bloc-voting faction of four is one player with weight 4.
  • A player's power is split among its members by weight. A coalition's power is the sum over its members, so overlapping coalitions can add up to more than 1.
  • Overlap. In Merge mode, a member in two bloc-voting coalitions links them into one bloc, since that member must vote with both. In Primary mode, the member votes with the first coalition listed. In both modes, they count towards each coalition's majority conditions.

Voting behaviour inside a coalition

  • Bloc: one player. Independent: every member is a player.
  • k sub-factions: members are split as evenly as possible into k groups (or into explicit sizes such as 2,1,1), and each group votes together.
  • N vote together: a core of N members is one bloc, and the rest vote on their own.
  • Probability p (whole coalition): with probability p the coalition is a bloc, otherwise every member votes on their own.
  • Probability p (each member): each member follows the coalition line with probability p. The followers form one bloc and defectors vote alone, so a coalition of m members has 2m possible structures.

For probabilistic behaviour the engine enumerates every voting structure across all coalitions, computes the exact index for each, and reports the probability-weighted average (the expected Shapley-Shubik power). If there are more than 4,096 structures it samples them. Exact enumeration handles up to 20 voting blocs per structure; beyond that the index is estimated from 40,000 random orderings.

Decision rules

A rule is a list of conditions that must all hold. Each condition looks at the yes-weight inside a scope (all members or one coalition) and requires it to be more than x%, at least x%, or at least x votes. This covers simple and special majorities, concurrent majorities ("majority of faculty"), affirmative-vote clauses (Tata's Article 121), Whole-Board thresholds with vacancies (give the vacant seat weight 0 and use a vote count), and supermajorities on several bodies at once (Anthropic's Trust Amendment Threshold).
